            Review the grammar you have studied, then practise one question at a time. Your results are saved on this device.

            1. To vs. -ing

            Some verbs change meaning depending on whether they are followed by the infinitive or the -ing form.

            • remember + -ing: remember a past action.
            • remember + to + verb: remember something you need to do.
            • stop + -ing: finish an activity.
            • stop + to + verb: pause one activity in order to do another.
            • try + to + verb: make an attempt.
            • try + -ing: experiment with a possible solution.
            ExamplesHe stopped checking his emails. / He stopped to check his emails.
            ExamplesI tried to restart my computer. / I tried restarting my computer.

            2. Active & Passive Voice

            In the active voice, the subject performs the action. In the passive voice, the subject receives the action.

            • Active: The HR team reviewed the structure.
            • Passive: The structure was reviewed by the HR team.
            • Present simple passive: am/is/are + past participle.
            • Past simple passive: was/were + past participle.
            • Present perfect passive: has/have been + past participle.
            • Future passive: will be + past participle.
            ExampleThe policy has been updated.

            3. Conditionals

            Choose the conditional according to whether you are talking about a general truth, a real possibility, an unreal present/future situation, or an unreal past situation.

            • Zero: if + present, present.
            • First: if + present, will + verb.
            • Second: if + past, would + verb.
            • Third: if + past perfect, would have + past participle.
            • Mixed: a past condition can have a present result, or a present condition can explain a past result.
            ExampleIf I had accepted that job, I would live in London now.

            4. Relative Pronouns

            Relative pronouns connect a noun to additional information about it.

            • who: people.
            • which: things.
            • whose: possession.
            • whom: a person as an object, especially after a preposition in formal English.
            • that: commonly used in defining clauses for people or things, but not after a comma in non-defining clauses.
            • where: places.
            ExamplesThe analyst who prepared the report is on leave. / The policy, which was introduced last year, has been updated.

            5. In vs. On vs. At

            Use the preposition according to the type of time or place you are describing.

            • in: countries, cities, years, months, parts of the day.
            • on: days, dates, and surfaces.
            • at: specific clock times and specific points/locations.
            Examplesin Brazil · in London · in 2012 · in the evening · on Monday · on the table · at 9 a.m. · at the entrance

            6. To vs. For

            Both can express relationships of purpose, direction or benefit, but they are not interchangeable.

            • to: destination or recipient — send it to me; go to London.
            • for: benefit or intended use — a gift for you; a room for meetings.
            • for + -ing: purpose/function — useful for analysing data.
            • to + verb: purpose — to improve the process.
            ExamplesI sent the report to my manager. / This tool is useful for analysing data.
